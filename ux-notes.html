UX notes

How the prototype is built and why. Written for whoever designs and builds the next version.

The helper is a new employee

Sal hires Rosa, gives her a phone, tells her the rules. No workflows, nodes or triggers ever appear. Her badge is the main object in the product.

The builder does the hard part

It reads the website, picks a local number, registers texting, guesses defaults. Sal only confirms. Complexity moves into the product, not onto the user.

One question at a time

Each step is a single card inside the conversation, with a recommended answer already picked and a "later" option.

Never a dead end

Every step has a way out: talk instead of type, bring in family, finish later, skip. Nothing is permanent; everything can be turned off.

Design system

"Main Street": clean and bright, like a well-kept shop on a sunny street. Real photos of owners at work carry the warmth; the interface stays white and quiet around them, so a 71-year-old trusts it with a phone line and a bank feed.

The studio

Where an owner and their team look after every helper. Built around one idea: a helper is a new employee the whole team manages together, so the studio borrows from how small teams already work: a group text, a shared to-do list, a sticky note on the fridge.

DecisionWhy
"Needs someone" comes firstThe only thing an owner must act on sits at the top of the workspace, marked in marigold (Von Restorff). Everything else is tracking.
Today in a sentenceEach helper's day is one plain sentence, with a small 7-day line beside it. No charts to decode (Miller's law).
Drafts, then publishEdits from anyone are saved as a draft; the helper keeps using the live version until someone presses Publish. Every version can be restored in one click, so nobody is afraid to try a change.
Edit in plain wordsA helper's job, greeting, tone and rules are written like instructions to a person. A live practice chat beside the editor shows the effect before publishing.
Helpers suggest their own fixes"4 people asked about Sundays. Add it?" turns gaps into one-tap improvements (Tesler's law: the product does the noticing).
Comments where the work isTeam notes sit on the exact rule or request they're about, with @mentions that arrive as texts, because that's where an older owner and his grandson already talk.
Four roles in plain wordsOwner, can change helpers, can handle requests, can only look. Access can be limited to certain helpers (an accountant sees only money helpers).
Invite by textPeople join with a link and a text-message code. No passwords to forget.
Who hears about whatEach kind of alert goes to the right people (an upset customer to Sal and Ana, the monthly report to Sal and Dana), so the owner isn't the bottleneck.
Presence"Marco is editing" and "Marco is typing" prevent two people changing the same thing and make a small team feel together.

Designing for a 71-year-old

  • Text never smaller than 15px; body at 19px; contrast at or above 7:1 for body text.
  • A microphone next to every text box. Speaking is the default path, not an extra.
  • No technical words anywhere in the product: no integration, workflow, trigger, webhook, API, deploy. It's "let Rosa use your calendar" and "put Rosa to work".
  • Every permission says in plain words what the helper will and won't do, before Sal agrees.
  • "Get help from family" is always one tap away and gives the helper limited access only.
  • Status never relies on color alone: every colored pill also says "On", "Off" or "Waiting on Marco".
  • Respects reduced-motion settings; motion only marks what just changed.

Hidden setup work the product has to absorb

This is where competitors leave users stuck. Each line is one tap for Sal and a real job for the product. Requirements change; verify each with the provider before building.

Sal seesWhat has to happen behind it
"Give her a new local number"Buy a number from a phone provider, point its calls and texts at the agent, set the caller name customers see.
"Send my shop's calls to her"Call forwarding codes and settings differ by carrier. Detect the carrier and show exact steps, or offer to move the number over.
Business textingIn the US, businesses texting from a regular 10-digit number generally need carrier registration (A2P 10DLC) or messages get filtered. Approval can take days, so calls should work before texts do, and the UI must say so.
WhatsAppUses Meta's WhatsApp Business Platform: business verification and display name approval. A number used there can't also be in the regular WhatsApp app.
Her own emailCreate a mailbox on the product's domain with proper sender authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) so her mail isn't marked as spam.
Google CalendarSign-in with Google asking for the narrowest access possible. Broader access scopes can require Google's app review.
Chat on the websiteA small code snippet, or a plugin for site builders. Most owners can't do this themselves: send instructions to their web person.
Facebook and InstagramMeta sign-in with page messaging permissions; the app needs Meta's review before going public.
TelegramA bot account created through Telegram's bot API. The simplest channel to turn on.
VoiceSpeech recognition, the agent, and a natural voice, all fast enough to feel like a person. Some US states require everyone's consent to record calls, so the greeting may need a recording notice.
"She knows it's you"Recognizing Sal by caller ID or email alone is spoofable. Ask for a short PIN or a confirmation text before anything involving money or deleting.

Open questions for the next round

  • Pricing Sal can understand: a flat monthly price per helper with minutes included, rather than credits.
  • What happens when Rosa gets something wrong in front of a customer, and how Sal finds out.
  • How much a family member can do: watch only, or change rules too.
  • Spending limits: below what amount can Rosa act without asking?
  • The second helper: does Rosa take on more jobs, or does Sal hire someone new?
